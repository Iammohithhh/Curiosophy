**Paper:** Kirkpatrick et al., *Pushing the frontiers of density functionals by solving the fractional electron problem* (2021). [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abj6511) · [author-hosted paper and supplement](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/papers/Data_Driven_Density_Functional_Design/data_driven_density_functional_design_unformatted.pdf).

## TL;DR

DM21 combines a neural local hybrid with training examples constructed around fractional charge and fractional spin. The important move is to teach known failure modes directly, rather than hoping ordinary molecular energies will constrain them indirectly.

## The problem

Separate a molecule into fragments. Where should an extra electron go? Some approximate functionals lower their energy artificially by spreading it fractionally across distant fragments. Stretch a bond while retaining spin symmetry, and a different problem appears: several electronic configurations become important.

These are not merely rare numerical accidents. They connect to exact properties of the energy as electron number and spin occupations vary.

## The key idea

A fractional electron number describes an **ensemble**, not a physically sliced electron. Between adjacent integer ground states, the exact ensemble energy lies on the straight line

$$E(N+\alpha)=(1-\alpha)E(N)+\alpha E(N+1),\qquad0\leq\alpha\leq1.$$

The slope is constant within that segment. Adjacent segments can have different slopes at the integer. Many approximations curve below the segment, making distributed fractional charge too favourable.

For an especially simple thought experiment, take two far-separated identical fragments with one added electron shared between them. If each fragment's approximate energy bows below the exact straight segment, two half-electron fragments can be artificially cheaper than one neutral and one charged fragment. The error at a fraction becomes a wrong preference for a real spatial distribution.

Fractional spin is different. Mixtures of degenerate spin states should have the same energy. Violating this constancy contributes to errors in spin-restricted bond dissociation. Fixing charge curvature alone does not automatically fix this spin behaviour.

## How it works

DM21 integrates local energy contributions predicted by a multilayer perceptron. Its inputs include local density ingredients and orbital-derived exact-exchange information, including a range-separated exchange contribution. Because some input features already encode nonlocal orbital interactions, “local network” does not mean “semilocal functional.” The paper describes the construction as a local range-separated hybrid.

The training collection contains 2,235 reactions: 1,161 molecular examples and 1,074 examples involving fractional charge and spin on atoms. Molecular density inputs are generated using B3LYP, while the energy labels use higher-accuracy references. The model learns energy differences, rather than being asked to imitate B3LYP energies.

The objective combines energy regression with gradient regularization intended to make the functional usable self-consistently. This is distinct from unrolling every SCF step as in [[papers/kohn-sham-regularizer|Li et al.]] The fractional examples encourage the desired energy surface; they do not constitute an analytic proof of the exact condition for every possible density.

## Results that matter

The paper tests molecular energetics alongside deliberately difficult charge-transfer and bond-dissociation cases. Comparisons between models trained with different physical constraints help isolate the importance of fractional-charge and fractional-spin examples. The compelling evidence is the combination: better behaviour on known pathologies without simply abandoning ordinary chemistry.

For reading, begin with the fractional-electron diagrams before the benchmark tables. Ask which direction through charge–spin space each experiment tests. A good energy on an integer, equilibrium molecule may tell us little about the slopes and curvature between those familiar examples.

## Limitations

DM21 retains exact-exchange ingredients, so it does not remove the conventional hybrid bottleneck merely by using a network. Its successes are demonstrated on finite tests; the title's “solving” should not be read as a universal proof for every molecule, solid or strongly correlated regime.

Training on physically constructed data also differs from imposing a condition identically through architecture. A finite penalty can be traded against other errors during optimization. That distinction matters when comparing DM21 with a constraint-built model such as [[papers/cider|CIDER]].

## My take

This paper changes what counts as useful training data. A deliberately constructed fractional system can expose a failure more clearly than another ordinary equilibrium molecule. It is a reminder to choose examples for what they teach the model about physics, not only for how many examples can be collected.
